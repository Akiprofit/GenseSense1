What Are GMOs? Understanding Genetic Modification

Published: 2024 Science Fundamentals

What Is a GMO?

A genetically modified organism (GMO) is a living thing whose genetic material has been artificially manipulated in a laboratory to achieve desired traits. This manipulation involves taking genes from one organism and inserting them into another—often across species boundaries—in ways that would not occur naturally.

In agriculture, GMOs most commonly refer to crop plants that have been genetically engineered to express specific characteristics: resistance to herbicides, tolerance to drought, pest resistance, or enhanced nutrition. GMO crops have been grown commercially since the mid-1990s and now represent a significant portion of global agricultural production, particularly in North America, South America, and parts of Asia.

Key Fact: Over 90% of corn, soybeans, and cotton grown in the United States are genetically modified varieties. Globally, GMO crops are grown on more than 190 million hectares of land across nearly 30 countries.

How Are GMOs Created?

Genetic modification in agriculture involves several steps and techniques, each refined over decades of research. The most common approaches are:

Agrobacterium-Mediated Transformation

Scientists use a naturally occurring bacterium called Agrobacterium tumefaciens as a delivery vehicle. This bacterium naturally transfers DNA into plant cells; scientists harness this ability to introduce engineered genes. The bacterium is loaded with a desired gene (such as a gene conferring herbicide resistance), and plant cells are exposed to the bacterium. The genes are incorporated into the plant's DNA, creating a transgenic plant that carries the new trait. This method works particularly well for dicots (two-leaf plants like soybeans and cotton).

Gene Gun (Particle Bombardment)

In this method, scientists coat microscopic gold or tungsten particles with DNA sequences containing the desired gene. A "gene gun" fires these particles at high velocity into plant cells. Some particles penetrate the cell nucleus, and the DNA can integrate into the plant's genome. This technique is often used for monocots (grasses like corn and rice) and has proven effective for crops that resist Agrobacterium infection.

DNA Microinjection

Scientists use a fine micropipette to inject DNA directly into a cell nucleus. While precise, this method is labor-intensive and impractical for large-scale crop development. It is primarily used in research contexts and for creating transgenic animals rather than food crops.

Important Note: Genetic modification is distinct from traditional gene editing techniques like CRISPR. While CRISPR is faster and more precise, most commercially available GMO crops were created using the transformation methods described above.

GMOs vs. Traditional Breeding: Understanding the Difference

A common question is: how is genetic modification different from traditional crop breeding? The answer lies in method, speed, and precision—not in safety or fundamental mechanism.

Traditional Breeding

For thousands of years, farmers have improved crops by selecting seeds from plants with desirable traits—larger seeds, drought resistance, better taste—and replanting them. This process, called selective breeding or artificial selection, involves crossing two plants and selecting offspring that display the desired combination of traits. The process is:

  • Slow (takes 8–12 years to develop a new variety)
  • Random (crosses entire genomes; unintended traits may be inherited)
  • Limited to related species (wheat can be crossed with closely related grasses, but not with animal genes)
  • Cumulative (combines many genes simultaneously)

Genetic Modification

Genetic modification works by:

  • Identifying a specific gene responsible for a desired trait
  • Extracting that gene from a source organism (plant, bacterium, or even animal)
  • Inserting it into the target crop plant's DNA
  • Creating a plant that expresses that specific trait

This process is:

  • Fast (5–8 years to develop a new variety)
  • Precise (targets a single gene or small number of genes)
  • Crosses species barriers (a bacterial gene can be inserted into corn)
  • Deliberate (only the intended traits are modified)

A Key Similarity

Both traditional breeding and genetic modification result in organisms with new combinations of genes. The fundamental biological outcome—a plant carrying genetic material it would not naturally possess—is similar. The difference is in precision and method. Traditional breeding produces many unexpected genetic changes alongside the desired trait; genetic modification targets specific genes. Neither approach is inherently safer than the other; each has distinct advantages and limitations.

Why Understanding GMO Science Matters

The science of GMOs is essential context for conversations about health, ethics, and society. Here's why:

For Health Discussions: Understanding how GMOs are created helps clarify what they are and aren't. A GMO is not "filled with chemicals"—it is an organism with modified DNA. The safety of a GMO depends on the specific trait introduced, the regulatory testing it has undergone, and its agronomic properties—not on the fact that it is genetically modified.

For Ethical Discussions: Knowing the technical difference between breeding and modification clarifies ethical questions. If we accept traditional breeding (which has produced countless unintended genetic changes over millennia), the ethics of genetic modification hinge on specific traits, ecological impacts, and regulatory oversight—not on genetic modification itself.

For Societal Conversations: Scientific literacy enables informed participation in policy discussions about agricultural technology, food systems, and innovation. Whether one supports or opposes GMO adoption, understanding the science enables more productive dialogue with researchers, policymakers, and farmers.

Core Takeaway: GMOs are a tool for agriculture with specific advantages (precision, speed, crossing species barriers) and limitations (regulatory complexity, ecological considerations). Whether and how to use this tool depends on scientific evidence and value-based choices about food systems, corporate power, environmental stewardship, and global equity—topics explored in later articles.
