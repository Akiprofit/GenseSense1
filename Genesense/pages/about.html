About GeneSense

GeneSense is an educational research project exploring the ethical implications of GMOs to human health and society. We transform rigorous research into accessible knowledge.

Our Approach

Research-Based: Every claim on GeneSense is backed by credible sources—peer-reviewed research, government data, and scientific consensus.

Balanced: We present multiple perspectives on ethical questions. GMOs aren't "good" or "bad"—they're a technology with genuine benefits and real concerns.

Accessible: We translate complex science and policy into language anyone can understand, without oversimplifying.

Practical: We explore real-world case studies to show how GMOs actually work in farming communities and food systems.

Our Research Topic

Ethical Implications of GMOs to Human Health and Society

This project was developed for a Science, Technology, and Society (STS) course. STS explores how science and technology shape society—and how society shapes science and technology. The GMO conversation is fundamentally an STS question: it's about science, ethics, economics, policy, and what kind of food system we want to build.

How This Website Was Built

Content: Research synthesized from peer-reviewed literature, government agricultural agencies, international organizations (FAO, WHO, CGIAR), and academic institutions.

Structure: The website is organized to support learning: start with foundational articles, then explore specific angles (health, ethics, society). Case studies ground abstract concepts in real experience. The interactive quiz reinforces learning.

Technology: Built with HTML, CSS, and JavaScript to ensure the site is accessible, fast, and works on any device.

Research Methodology and Scope

This project uses a descriptive, literature-based, qualitative-analytical approach. It reviews and organizes existing research, institutional reports, government publications, and regulatory materials rather than conducting laboratory experiments, clinical trials, or field interviews.

The paper identifies peer-reviewed studies, publications from government and regulatory bodies, reports from scientific and international health organizations such as WHO and NASEM, and academic literature as information sources. Topics are organized around human health, ethical questions, societal impacts, case studies, and emerging gene-editing debates.

Scope limitation: GeneSense is an educational summary of secondary sources. It is not original experimental research, medical advice, or a substitute for reviewing the cited sources directly.

Data Gathering Procedure

  1. Search academic journals and official WHO, NASEM, PhilRice, and Council of the European Union sources using terms related to GMO crops, food safety, labeling, and Golden Rice.
  2. Check author, publisher, source reliability, and publication date; prioritize recent sources for current issues while retaining foundational references.
  3. Organize findings under research objectives: GMO science, health and food safety, ethics, social and environmental effects, case studies, and governance.
  4. Compare findings across sources, note agreement and disagreement, and represent different positions fairly.
  5. Synthesize findings into answers to research questions, then review accuracy and citations before publishing educational content.

Website Development Plan

GeneSense presents the reviewed findings as educational articles about GMO science, human health, benefits and risks, ethics, and social impacts. Dedicated case-study content explains Philippine agricultural biotechnology and the Golden Rice debate. The glossary defines key terms; the references page links source material; visual sections organize complex ideas; and the interactive quiz reinforces learning. HTML structures the pages, CSS maintains the existing visual design and responsive layout, and JavaScript supports interactive features.

Website content is based on the paper’s literature review and should distinguish source-backed findings from unresolved questions. Each research claim should have a traceable citation. Section 3.4 of the supplied paper still contains a drafting instruction, so this website plan is a project implementation based on the current website rather than text copied from a completed paper section.

Sources & Methodology

How We Selected Sources

Every major claim on GeneSense is backed by at least one credible source. Our priority order:

  1. Peer-reviewed scientific journals
  2. Government regulatory agencies (FDA, EFSA, Health Canada, etc.)
  3. International organizations (WHO, FAO, UN)
  4. Academic institutions and research centers
  5. Major news organizations with editorial standards

We avoid: personal blogs, unvetted social media, low-quality content farms, and sources without transparent funding or author credentials.

How We Distinguish Evidence from Debate

Established Science: When we say "research shows," we mean peer-reviewed findings with broad scientific consensus. Example: "Approved GMO foods are safe to eat." This is backed by WHO, FDA, and numerous scientific studies.

Active Debate: When we say "some argue," "questions remain," or "there's concern about," we're noting areas where reasonable people disagree or evidence is incomplete. Example: "Long-term ecological effects of GMO crops require more research."

Our Opinion vs. Evidence: GeneSense doesn't take a position on whether GMOs should be adopted. We present evidence and let you think critically.

What We Don't Do

  • Invent studies or statistics (if we can't verify it, we don't claim it)
  • Exaggerate risks or benefits
  • Hide data that contradicts our narrative (because we don't have a pre-set narrative)
  • Rely solely on corporate-funded research without noting potential bias
  • Present fringe scientific views as mainstream consensus
